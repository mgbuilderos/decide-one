## The short answer

Say what it displaces, not whether you can cope.

"I can do that by Thursday if the report moves to next week — which would you rather?" is a different sentence from "I'll try to fit it in", and it is the only one that gives the other person the information they need. Most people asking are not trying to overload you; they cannot see your queue and are assuming there is room.

Saying no is not about assertiveness. It is **admission control** — deciding what enters the system — and every queue that stays responsive has some, because a queue with no entry rule accepts work until it collapses.

## Why "I'll try" is the expensive answer

It feels like the kind thing to say. It is the one that reliably produces the worst outcome for everybody.

**It transfers a decision without transferring the information.** The other person now believes the work is handled. They stop looking for another route, and they find out it is not handled at the point when it is too late to do anything.

**It makes the cost invisible.** Something else will be later because of this. "I'll try" hides which thing, so nobody gets to weigh it, and the trade happens by accident rather than by choice.

**It accepts the work into the queue regardless.** By Little's Law, average completion time is proportional to how much is in progress — so a maybe has already made everything else slower. [The arithmetic is here](/guides/littles-law-for-a-working-day/).

## The sentences that work

**Name the displacement.** *"Yes — and that means X moves to Friday. Is that the right trade?"* This is not a refusal at all. It is handing the decision to the person who is allowed to make it, with the facts attached.

**Give a real date instead of a no.** *"I can start this on the 14th."* Most requests are less urgent than their phrasing, and a specific future date is accepted far more often than people expect.

**Offer the smaller version.** *"I can't do the full analysis this week. I can give you the three numbers you actually need by tomorrow."* Often the smaller version was all that was wanted.

**Ask what it is competing with.** *"I've got the migration and the board pack this week — where does this sit against those?"* If the answer is "above both", that is useful and you have just been reprioritised rather than overloaded.

**Say no plainly when it is a no.** *"I can't take that on."* No elaborate justification: a long explanation invites negotiation of each reason in turn, and it implies the answer is up for debate when it is not.

## The part people get wrong

**Do not negotiate the estimate.** If it takes two days, it takes two days. Agreeing to do it in one because the conversation is uncomfortable moves the failure from now — where it is cheap and can be planned around — to the deadline, where it is expensive and surprising.

**Do not apologise at length.** Apology signals that you did something wrong by having finite capacity. You did not.

**Do not say no to the person.** Say no to the timing, the scope, or the trade. *"Not this week"* keeps the relationship and closes the request.

## When you cannot say no

Sometimes the answer really is not yours. That is genuinely common and the advice above does not evaporate — it changes target.

Write down what you were asked, what it displaced, and what the actual outcome was. Not as a grievance file; as data. Two weeks of planned-against-actual is a far more persuasive conversation than "I'm overloaded", because it is checkable and it is about the work rather than about you.

And when the arithmetic does not fit, that is [a capacity fact rather than a character one](/guides/what-to-do-when-you-have-too-much-to-do/). No ordering rule creates hours.
