## The short answer

Do not rebuild the old plan. Start today.

The instinct after time away — a week, a month, longer — is to reconstruct where you were and resume from there. It rarely works, because the plan was written against a world that has moved, and because reconstructing it is a large, vague, unrewarding task standing between you and any progress at all.

The thing that works is smaller and slightly uncomfortable: **pick three things for today and do them.** Not to catch up. Just today. Catching up is not a task and cannot be started.

## Why the old plan is the wrong thing to restore

**It is out of date in ways you cannot see.** Some of it is done, some is irrelevant, some was overtaken. Sorting that out is itself hours of work, and it produces no output.

**It is the wrong size.** A plan built during a normal stretch assumes a normal amount of context loaded. On the first morning back you have none of that, and the honest capacity is lower than usual.

**It puts a large reconstruction task first**, on the day when starting anything is hardest. That is the opposite of what a first day back needs.

Keep the old list — it is a useful record of what once mattered. Just do not make reading it the first thing.

## The first morning, concretely

1. **Write down what is in your head**, for ten minutes, without sorting it. Everything you are half-remembering costs attention while it is unwritten and nothing once it is down.
2. **Find what is on fire**, if anything. Usually less than feared, and usually two things rather than twenty.
3. **Send the unblocking messages.** Anything waiting on you that someone else is stuck behind. Short, one ask each. This buys back more of the week than anything else you could do before lunch.
4. **Pick three things and give them times.** Only today. [Three is small enough to bind](/methods/top-3-method/), which is the point.
5. **Leave the backlog alone until tomorrow.** It will still be there and it will read better when you have one finished day behind you.

## About the gap itself

Worth saying plainly, because most software gets this wrong and it matters.

**A day you did not open is a day you did not open.** It is not a failure, it is not a streak broken, and nothing about it needs to be made up. An unfinished task from three weeks ago is unfinished — a description, not a verdict, and the calendar moving on does not turn one into the other.

If a tool greets you after a month away with a wall of red, it has decided something on your behalf that it has no standing to decide. That is a design choice, and it is the wrong one. The test any returning screen should pass: *could this make someone feel bad on a day they had a bad day?*

The useful question on the way back is not what you missed. It is what matters now — and that is answerable this morning, in ten minutes, without reconstructing anything.

## The second and third day

**Day two**, look at the old list properly. With one completed day behind you it reads differently: most items are obviously stale, a few are obviously live, and the sorting takes twenty minutes rather than feeling infinite.

**Day three**, go back to whatever your normal planning is. Not before — the first two days are for getting moving, and normal planning assumes a context you have not rebuilt yet.

Anything still on the list after a week away and a week back is worth a proper look. [It is usually one of four specific things](/guides/a-task-you-keep-postponing/), and none of them is a discipline problem.
