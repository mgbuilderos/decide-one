## The short answer

A task is well written when you can start it without deciding anything first.

Four properties, and most badly-behaved items fail the first:

1. **It names a first physical action.** *Draft the intro paragraph*, not *sort out the report*.
2. **It is estimable.** If you cannot put a rough time on it, it is not understood yet.
3. **It has one finish.** You can say whether it is done without interpreting.
4. **It is one task.** If it contains an "and", it is two.

The five-second test: **say the first thirty seconds out loud.** If you cannot, the wording is the problem — not your discipline, and not the priority.

## Why wording matters more than it sounds

A vague task makes you do two jobs at the moment you look at it: work out what it means, then do it. The first job has no obvious start and no visible progress, so it is the one that gets deferred — and the whole item stalls behind it.

This is why [a task can survive weeks of being scheduled later](/guides/a-task-you-keep-postponing/) while everything around it moves. Nothing was wrong with its priority. There was simply nothing to begin.

## The four properties, concretely

**A first physical action.** The test is whether it names something your hands do. *Email Priya asking for Q3 figures* passes. *Follow up with Priya* does not — it still requires deciding what "follow up" means, and that decision is the obstacle.

**Estimable.** Not accurate: estimable. If no rough figure comes to mind, you do not yet know what the task involves, and *find out what this involves* is the real first item. This is also what makes a day's arithmetic possible at all — [what an estimate is actually for](/guides/how-long-should-a-task-take/).

**One finish.** *Improve onboarding* has no end state, so it can absorb unlimited time and never be complete. *Cut the signup form to four fields* ends.

**One task.** "Write the deck and send it to legal" is two items with a dependency, and writing them as one hides the dependency — which is where blocked work goes to become invisible.

## What to do with the bad ones

Do not delete them. They are usually pointing at something real.

**Rewrite as the first action.** *Sort out the website* → *list the three pages that need changing*. That is startable, estimable and finishable, and it will produce the next real task.

**Split on the "and".** Two items, and note which blocks which.

**If you cannot name a first action, make that the task.** *Spend twenty minutes working out what the migration actually involves* is a perfectly good item. It is honest about being investigation rather than execution, and it ends.

**Delete the ones that are wishes.** *Be better at email* is not a task. Either it becomes a specific action or it is an intention, and an intention on a task list costs attention every read and returns nothing.

## The one exception

Not everything needs this much precision. A list of small, obvious, same-kind items — a shopping list, a batch of expenses — works fine as fragments, because the first action is self-evident.

Precision is worth the effort exactly where things stall: the item you have looked at four times and not started. That is where the wording is almost always the problem, and where a ninety-second rewrite outperforms another day of resolve.
