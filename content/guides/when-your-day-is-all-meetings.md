## The short answer

Four hours of gaps between meetings is not four hours of work, and planning as though it were is what makes those days feel like a fraud.

Each gap costs a reload — rebuilding what was pending, what you had ruled out, where you left it. A twenty-minute gap can be almost entirely reload, producing nothing. Three of them do not add up to an hour of anything.

So on a meeting-heavy day, plan for the **shape** of the time rather than its total. Small, closable, low-context work in the gaps. One real thing only if there is one real block.

## Why the gaps disappoint

**The reload is fixed, the gap is not.** Getting back into a substantial piece of work costs roughly the same whether you then have twenty minutes or two hours. In a twenty-minute gap that overhead is most of the gap.

**Meetings leave residue.** The five minutes after one is spent on the thing you just agreed to, which is real work but is not the work you planned.

**They do not start on time and they overrun.** A gap on the calendar is longer than the gap in reality, reliably and in one direction.

**The day is at full utilisation.** With no spare capacity, one meeting overrunning by ten minutes displaces everything after it — and [waiting time rises steeply as a system approaches full](/guides/should-you-schedule-every-hour/). A fully booked day has nowhere to put a slip.

## What to actually put in a gap

Match the work to the size honestly:

- **Under 20 minutes**: messages, approvals, the blocking two-minute replies, writing down where you left something. Not "make a start on" anything.
- **20–45 minutes**: one small task you can finish. Finishing is the point — an unfinished small task is another open item.
- **45+ minutes**: one real thing, but only if you name it before the day starts. Deciding what to do with a found hour usually consumes a good part of it.

**Write down where you were before every meeting.** Two lines: what was pending, what you had ruled out. Fifteen seconds, and it converts the next reload from five minutes into one. On a five-meeting day that is most of an hour recovered by a habit that costs a minute and a quarter in total.

## The thing worth doing before the day

**Name one thing.** One. If the day contains a single block, that block has a name decided in advance — not chosen at 2pm from whatever seems urgent then.

Everything else on a day like this is maintenance, and expecting otherwise is what makes the day feel like a failure rather than a busy day that went fine.

## Changing the shape, which is the real fix

Scheduling technique has a ceiling here, and it is low. Past a certain meeting load the honest problem is not how you use the gaps.

**Stack them.** The same five meetings back-to-back leaves one real block; spread evenly they leave nothing. This is usually the single biggest available improvement and it costs nothing but rescheduling.

**Protect one block a week the way a meeting is protected.** Something on a calendar is defended; something not on it is not. This is not a trick — it is using the only mechanism the calendar respects.

**Decline the ones where you are optional.** Ask what decision needs you. "Send me the notes" is an acceptable answer far more often than people expect.

**Show the arithmetic if it does not fit.** Two weeks of planned against actual is a checkable conversation. "I'm in too many meetings" is not, and it invites a discussion about you rather than the schedule.
