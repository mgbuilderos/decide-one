## The short answer

Do not sort it. Sorting two hundred items produces a ranking that is stale within a week and took an afternoon to build.

A backlog is not a plan and does not need a total order. It needs three things:

1. **A top**, which is short — the ten or so items that are genuinely next.
2. **A bin**, used regularly and without ceremony.
3. **Everything else left unsorted**, where it costs nothing.

The mistake is reading the backlog as a queue to be ordered. It is a **pool to be chosen from**, and the choosing happens at the day.

## Why a full ranking is wasted work

**Most of it will never be done.** That is not a failure of the backlog; it is what a backlog is. Ranking items 60 through 200 orders things that will be deleted or overtaken before they are reached.

**The order is wrong by next week.** Priorities that depend on the world change when the world does. A ranking is a snapshot presented as a plan.

**It feels like progress and produces none.** An afternoon of sorting ends with the same amount of work done as an afternoon of nothing, and a stronger feeling of having been productive — which is the more expensive of the two outcomes.

**Ranking cost scales badly.** Comparing two hundred items pairwise is not something anyone actually does, so what really happens is a rough sort by recency and guilt.

## What to do instead, in twenty minutes

**Pull the top ten to the surface.** Not ranked among themselves — just separated. These are what could plausibly be next. Everything else stays where it is.

**Delete without ceremony.** Anything older than three months that has never been close to the top is not going to be done. Deleting it is not giving up; it is removing something that costs attention every time the list is read and returns nothing. A backlog nobody deletes from becomes a record of intentions rather than a source of work.

**Merge the duplicates.** Long backlogs always contain the same thing written four ways at different times. Merging them is usually the single biggest reduction available.

**Tag only what is blocked, and on whom.** Not categories, not effort scores — just blocked or not. Blocked items cannot be next however important they are, and knowing that at a glance saves picking one by mistake.

That is the whole review. Twenty minutes, once every week or two.

## Why a long backlog is not itself the problem

Worth being precise, because guilt about backlog size drives a lot of wasted sorting.

By Little's Law, average completion time is proportional to **work in progress** — things started and unfinished. A captured item you have not started is not in progress and costs you nothing in throughput. A hundred unstarted items do not slow anything down. [The arithmetic.](/guides/littles-law-for-a-working-day/)

What slows you down is how many things are simultaneously open. So the discipline that matters is at the day — a small number, chosen and closed — not at the backlog, which can be as long as it needs to be. [More on list length.](/guides/is-a-long-to-do-list-bad/)

The exception: a backlog gets expensive when reading it costs something. If opening it produces dread rather than options, that is the signal to delete, not to sort.

## Choosing from it

Once a day, take a small number — [three is practical](/methods/top-3-method/) — from the top ten. The question is not "what is most important overall?" but "what most needs to be true by this evening?", which is a question the day can answer and the backlog cannot.

Anything that keeps reaching the top ten and never gets chosen is [telling you something specific](/guides/a-task-you-keep-postponing/), and it is usually not a discipline problem.
