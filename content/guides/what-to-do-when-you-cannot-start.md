## The short answer

Check whether there is actually a first action named anywhere.

Most of the time there is not. "Work on the proposal" is a heading, not something you can begin — there is no first physical move in it, so every attempt to start requires deciding what starting means, and that decision is the thing being avoided. The gap looks like reluctance from the inside. It is usually ambiguity.

The test takes five seconds: **can you say the first thirty seconds out loud?** *Open the document and write the section headings.* If you can, you are not stuck. If you cannot, you have found the problem.

## Why this is not a motivation failure

A day where the volume is fine but nothing moves is a different condition from a day holding too much, and it needs a different response. Naming the difference matters, because the advice for overload — do less, cut scope — does nothing here. There is capacity. There is no sequence.

The ordering rule that fixes it is boringly old. [The Ivy Lee method](/methods/ivy-lee-method/) is six tasks written in strict order the night before, worked top to bottom without renegotiating. Its value is not the number six; it is that at the moment of starting, the decision has already been made by a version of you who was not standing at the edge of it.

**Deciding what to do is itself work**, and it is the most expensive thing to do at the exact moment you are trying to begin.

## Five things that reliably work

1. **Name the first thirty seconds, in writing.** Not the task — the physical action. Open the file. Find the email. Write the first heading. If naming it is hard, that is the whole obstacle and you have now spent thirty seconds on it instead of a morning.
2. **Decide the order somewhere other than the starting line.** The evening before is the classic answer and it works for [a specific reason](/guides/plan-your-day-the-night-before/): it moves the choice away from the moment you are least able to make it.
3. **Start the clock before you feel ready.** A running timer is a commitment that a page is not. The point is not pressure; it is that the decision to begin has been converted into an event that already happened.
4. **Shrink until it is embarrassing.** If ninety minutes is unstartable, try ten. Ten minutes of a thing you have been avoiding for a week is a large improvement over the week, and the resistance is nearly always at the start rather than distributed through.
5. **Check it is not blocked.** Sometimes the reason you cannot start is that you genuinely cannot — something is missing and some part of you knows it. Thirty seconds of checking either removes the obstacle or turns it into a different, smaller task: *ask Dan for the numbers*.

## When it is avoidance rather than ambiguity

Sometimes the first action is perfectly clear and you still will not do it. That is real and it is worth being honest rather than pretending a technique dissolves it.

Two things are still useful. **Make it smaller until the resistance is not worth it** — the aversion usually scales with the imagined size, not the actual one. And **do it first**, before the day fills, because a thing you are avoiding never becomes more likely later; it just accumulates the weight of having been avoided all day.

What is not useful is reading the difficulty as evidence about you. This page will not do that, and neither should a tool.

## The one thing to stop doing

**Stop reorganising the list.** Sorting, re-sorting, moving things between apps and rewriting the plan all feel like progress and are the most common way a morning disappears. The list is not what is stuck.

If you have caught yourself tidying the plan, that is the signal to name the first thirty seconds and start the clock.
