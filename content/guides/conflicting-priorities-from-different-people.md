## The short answer

This is not your conflict to resolve, and attempting to resolve it privately is what makes it expensive.

Two people have each ranked their own work first. Both rankings are reasonable from where they sit. Neither can see the other's, and neither can see your queue. The only person who currently knows a conflict exists is you — and the useful move is to stop being the only one.

**Make it visible, with numbers, and let them decide.** *"I have A from you and B from Priya, both due Thursday. Together they are about three days of work and there are two. Which goes first?"*

That is not escalation and it is not complaining. It is returning a decision to the people who are allowed to make it.

## Why absorbing it silently fails

**You will pick wrong roughly half the time**, because you do not have the information. Relative importance across two people's work is not something you can see from your seat.

**Both of them keep planning as if their work is on track.** The conflict does not disappear when you absorb it; it surfaces later, as a surprise, when it is more expensive and when it does look like your failure.

**It sets the precedent that you have unlimited capacity.** If competing requests always get absorbed, the requests keep arriving, because nothing in the system is telling anyone otherwise.

**It makes the cost invisible.** Something will be late. Absorbing the conflict just means nobody knows which thing until it is.

## How to raise it without it being a complaint

**Lead with the arithmetic, not the feeling.** *"Three days of work, two days available"* is checkable. *"I'm overloaded"* is a claim about you, and it invites a conversation about you rather than about the work.

**Name both items and both dates.** Specificity is what makes it solvable. Vagueness is what makes it sound like a grievance.

**Propose a resolution.** *"Unless you'd rather otherwise, I'll do A first and B lands Monday."* Most of the time this is simply accepted, and you have resolved it without anyone needing a meeting. The default is doing the work — you are asking for an objection, not a decision.

**Ask them to talk to each other** if it persists. Two or three conflicts in a month is a structural problem between them, not a scheduling problem for you, and it should be solved where it lives.

**Do it in writing.** Not defensively — because a written trade-off is one both people can check later, and because it stops the resolution being remembered differently by each of them.

## What to do while you wait for an answer

Do not stall. Start on whichever is **blocked for someone else** — work holding up another person is worth more than the same work in isolation, and that criterion is usually uncontroversial to both parties. [More on dependencies.](/guides/when-your-work-depends-on-others/)

If neither blocks anyone, start on the **reversible** one. If the decision comes back the other way, you have lost less.

## When there is genuinely no answer

Sometimes nobody will arbitrate, and both people carry on expecting to be first.

At that point the honest position is to pick, say clearly which you picked and what that means for the other, and hold it. *"A is first, so B lands Monday"* — communicated to both, in writing, before Thursday rather than on it.

That is not insubordination. It is the only remaining way to make the trade-off explicit, and someone always makes it — the choice is whether it is made openly on Monday or discovered on Thursday.

And if the arithmetic genuinely does not fit under any ordering, no method fixes that. [It is a capacity problem](/guides/how-to-say-no-to-more-work/), and the only lever is scope, time, or people.
