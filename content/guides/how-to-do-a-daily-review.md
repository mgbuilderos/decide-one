## The short answer

Four questions, five minutes, at the end of the day rather than the start of the next one:

1. **What did I plan, and what actually happened?**
2. **Where did the time actually go?**
3. **What is blocked, and on whom?**
4. **What are tomorrow's first three?**

That is the whole thing. Its value is almost entirely in question one, because comparing planned against actual is the only mechanism by which a guess becomes an estimate.

## Why it goes at the end of the day

**You still have the information.** At five o'clock you know what happened, what got in the way, and what is now waiting. Tomorrow morning you are reconstructing that from memory, badly and with a bias towards whatever is most recent.

**It closes the day.** Writing down where things stand is what makes it reasonable to stop thinking about them. The tasks are somewhere; they do not need to be carried home.

**It sets tomorrow's first move.** A morning that opens with a written first line opens with work. A morning that opens with "what should I do first?" opens with deliberation, at the hour when deliberation is most expensive. [The reasoning in full.](/guides/plan-your-day-the-night-before/)

## What each question is for

**Planned against actual** is the engine. Not to score the day — to find where your estimates are systematically wrong. That is almost always a small, specific set of categories rather than everything: for most people one or two kinds of work are reliably underestimated and the rest are roughly fine. You cannot find that pattern without writing the estimate down first, even a poor one.

**Where the time went** catches the things no list records. The reload after every switch, the meeting that ran long, the forty minutes on something that was never on any plan. These are what make a day feel full and look empty, and they are invisible unless asked about directly.

**What is blocked, and on whom** is the highest-return question per second spent. Something waiting on another person does not move however you rank it, and the unblocking action is usually a two-minute message — best sent now, while you still remember exactly what you need. [More on dependencies.](/guides/when-your-work-depends-on-others/)

**Tomorrow's first three** is the output. Three, with durations, decided now rather than in the morning.

## The thing that must never be in it

**A verdict.**

The review is an instrument reading, not an assessment. "Two of three done, the third blocked on legal, the estimate for the deck was out by an hour" is useful. "Bad day, must do better" is not information and it makes the review something you will stop doing within a fortnight.

This matters more than it sounds. A review that grades you gets avoided on exactly the days it would be most useful — the difficult ones — so the data goes missing precisely where the pattern lives. An unfinished task is unfinished. That is a description of a state, not a judgement about a person, and the distinction is what keeps the habit alive.

## Making it survive

**Five minutes, not twenty.** A twenty-minute review is done for a week. A five-minute one is done for a year, and a year of five-minute reviews is worth far more.

**Same trigger every day.** Attached to something that already happens — closing the laptop, the last coffee. A habit with no trigger is an intention.

**Write it somewhere you will see it tomorrow.** A review whose output you never read again is a diary, not an instrument.

**Skip it when the day has genuinely ended badly.** Then do it the next evening. Enforcing it on a bad day is how it becomes the thing being avoided rather than the thing that helps.
