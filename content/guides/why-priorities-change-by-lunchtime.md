## The short answer

Because nothing stops it. A plan that can be reopened at any moment is not a plan; it is a suggestion competing with every new arrival, and new arrivals are louder than a decision you made at nine o'clock.

The fix is not more resolve. It is a rule about **when the order is allowed to change** — and the answer that works is "at fixed points, not continuously".

{{diagram:ivy-lee-order}}

## What is actually happening

Two mechanisms, and both are structural rather than personal.

**Everything that arrives after the plan is made looks more urgent than the plan.** Not because it is, but because it is new and it is asking now. Urgency is about *when* and importance is about *consequence*, and only one of them makes a noise. A morning decision competes all day against items with the unfair advantage of having just appeared.

**Re-deciding is cheap in the moment and expensive in aggregate.** Each reshuffle takes a minute and feels responsible. Six of them cost the day, because every one is also a switch, and [a switch costs the reload rather than the minute](/guides/the-cost-of-switching-tasks/).

By lunchtime the order bears no relation to the morning's, and nobody made that decision. It happened one reasonable exception at a time.

## The scheduling idea that transfers

Operating systems separate two things people run together: **whether work can be interrupted**, and **whether the ordering can be renegotiated**.

A **non-preemptive** scheduler will not interrupt a running task — once something has the processor it keeps it until it yields. That is the strongest form and it has an obvious cost: genuinely urgent things wait.

The workable compromise is **a minimum run before anything can displace you**. The problem is rarely that the order changes; it is that it changes before anything has reached a state worth keeping.

[The Ivy Lee method](/methods/ivy-lee-method/) is a non-preemptive discipline written in 1918: six tasks in order, worked strictly top to bottom, and the order is not revisited during the day. Its whole value is the sentence people skip — *do not reshuffle*. Without that it is just a numbered list.

## A rule that survives a real week

The original is stricter than most modern jobs can carry, and a rule abandoned by Wednesday is worse than a slightly looser one that holds. What works:

1. **Decide the order once, outside the day.** The evening before is the classic answer and it works because it moves the decision away from the moment you are least able to make it well.
2. **Allow exactly one revision, in the morning, once.** Read the list, change it if the world genuinely changed overnight, then commit. One revision absorbs news. Continuous revision means the order never meant anything.
3. **Finish the current item before reordering anything.** Not the whole plan — the current item. This is the minimum-run rule, and it is the single most effective of the five.
4. **Park arrivals; do not rank them.** New work goes on the capture list, not into today's order. It gets considered at the next fixed point. Most of it turns out not to be urgent once it has waited ninety minutes.
5. **Name the two things that genuinely interrupt.** For most people it is a very short list. Everything not on it waits, and knowing in advance what qualifies is what stops each arrival re-opening the question.

## The real test

If the plan changes, ask: **did something change, or did something arrive?**

Something changing — a deadline moved, a dependency resolved, a decision reversed — is a legitimate reason to re-plan, and a plan that cannot absorb real news is too rigid to use.

Something arriving is not. An arrival is only evidence that work exists, which you already knew.

Nearly every lunchtime reshuffle is the second kind wearing the clothes of the first.
