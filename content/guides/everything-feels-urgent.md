## The short answer

Sort every item on two separate questions before you start any of them: **is it urgent**, and **is it important**. Urgent means time is running out. Important means the outcome changes. They are different questions, they usually have different answers, and answering them together is why the day disappears.

Do the urgent-and-important now. Schedule the important-and-not-urgent with a real time. Contain the urgent-and-not-important. Drop the rest.

## Why they feel like the same question

Urgency has a deadline attached, so it produces a physical pull. Importance does not. A message that arrived thirty seconds ago feels more pressing than work that will decide the quarter, because the message is *making a sound* and the work is not.

That asymmetry is not a failure of attention. It is what urgency is for — it exists to get things noticed. The problem is only that nothing plays the same role for importance, so importance has to be given one deliberately, in advance, in writing.

## Two named failure modes

Both of these have fifty years of literature behind them, and both describe an ordinary week precisely.

**Starvation** is when low-priority work never runs because higher-priority work keeps arriving. Nobody decided the important thing would not happen. It simply never reached the front of the queue, because something more urgent kept arriving in front of it. This is the single most common shape of a wasted quarter.

**Priority inversion** is subtler and worse: a low-priority task holds a resource that a high-priority task needs, so the important work is blocked by the trivial work. In 1997 the Mars Pathfinder rover kept resetting on the surface of Mars for exactly this reason — a low-priority meteorological task held a lock that a high-priority bus-management task needed. NASA diagnosed it from Earth and patched the scheduler remotely.

In a working week, priority inversion is the afternoon spent waiting on a two-minute approval from someone who is in meetings. The important work is not slow. It is blocked by something small.

## What to do when the answer really is "all of it"

Sometimes the sort genuinely returns four urgent-and-important items and a day that holds two.

**Say so, early, to whoever is affected.** This is the actual answer and most advice avoids it because it is uncomfortable rather than because it is wrong. A list does not create hours. Deciding which two happen — and telling people the other two are moving — is a smaller cost than discovering it at 6pm.

**Then pick by what unblocks the most.** Work that other work is waiting behind costs more than its own duration, because everything queued behind it is also waiting. That is not a judgement about which task deserves more; it is arithmetic about total delay.

## The quadrant that decides everything

Important and not urgent is the one that gets eaten, every time, and it is the one that decides whether next month is calmer or the same.

It needs more than intention. The one research finding this site cites belongs precisely here: Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis of **implementation intentions** — deciding in advance *when* and *where* you will do something, in an if-then form — found a medium-to-large effect on follow-through, d = 0.65.

That result is about if-then planning specifically. It is not a validation of any matrix, of prioritisation in general, or of any product. What it establishes is narrow and useful: "schedule it" has to mean a time and a place written down, not a resolution to get to it.

## Three rules that keep the sort from becoming the work

1. **Classify fast.** One pass, first instinct. A task you spend four minutes categorising is a task you could have started.
2. **Be strict about important.** If everything lands in important, the axis has stopped carrying information. The honest question is what actually changes if this is never done.
3. **Stop sorting and start.** The matrix is a decision, not a workspace.
