## How the Top 3 method works

Write the three things that matter most today. Do not write a fourth. Work through them in whatever order the day allows, and judge the day on those three alone.

That is the whole method. The hard part is not the writing — it is accepting that the fourth thing does not get a line.

## Why three, and what the number actually rests on

There is a real result underneath this, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not say.

**Little's Law**, published by John Little in 1961, describes any stable queueing system:

> L = λW — the average number of items in the system equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each item spends there.

Rearranged, average completion time is proportional to work in progress. **Halve the number of things in flight and you halve how long each one takes.** This is why Kanban boards cap work in progress, and a work-in-progress limit is the Top 3 method applied to a team.

**Now the honest part.** Little's Law is a theorem about queueing systems. It is not a finding about human cognition, and it names no number. It does not say you can only do three things. Nobody has proven that, and this page will not claim it.

What it does establish is the direction: fewer things in flight means each one finishes sooner. Three is a practical choice — small enough to force the decision, large enough that a day still has shape. If four works better for you, the arithmetic does not object. The discipline is having a limit at all.

## The failure this is built against

The problem has a formal name. In scheduling theory, **starvation** is what happens when low-priority work never runs because higher-priority work keeps arriving.

That is an ordinary week described exactly. The important thing is not forgotten and not deprioritised — it simply never reaches the front, because something more urgent keeps arriving in front of it. A list with twenty lines does not fix this. It records it.

Three lines and no fourth is a crude fix, and crude is the point: it forces the choice to happen at the moment you are calm enough to make it, rather than at 4pm when you are not.

## How to actually choose the three

1. **Write everything competing for the day first.** All of it, without filtering. You cannot choose between things you have not seen, and the list is usually shorter than it felt.
2. **Ask what would make today count** if nothing else got done. Not what is loudest.
3. **Take three.** If two feel equal, take the one that unblocks something else — work that other work is waiting behind costs more than its own duration.
4. **Give each one a duration.** A line without a time is a wish. The estimate does not need to be right; it needs to exist, so that at the end of the day you can compare what you planned against what happened.
5. **Leave the rest visible but unchosen.** Not deleted. In scheduling terms this is **aging** — what cannot run now moves up for next time. Nothing is lost by not being chosen today.

## When Top 3 is the wrong method

It fits one specific problem: taking on more than a day holds. If that is not your problem, a different structure fits better.

| What today actually feels like | The method that fits |
| :--- | :--- |
| Too much to do | Top 3 |
| I keep starting things and not finishing them | [The Ivy Lee method](/methods/ivy-lee-method/) |
| Everything feels urgent and I cannot tell what matters | [The urgent/important matrix](/methods/urgent-important-matrix/) |

This is not a preference. There is a known result in scheduling theory that **no single scheduling discipline is best across all objectives** — shortest-job-first minimises average waiting time, earliest-deadline-first is optimal for meeting deadlines (Liu and Layland, 1973), and neither beats the other in general. The right method depends on what today is actually asking for. Choosing before you know the problem is how people end up blaming themselves for a mismatch. [How to prioritise your tasks](/guides/how-to-prioritise-tasks/) walks through telling them apart.

## Where it came from

Unlike the other two methods, Top 3 has no single author. "Rule of three" is a general pattern found in writing, design and rhetoric, and the productivity version has been arrived at independently many times. There is no origin story to tell, and inventing one would be worse than saying so.
